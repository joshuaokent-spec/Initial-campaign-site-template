For Lansing

Government for People, Not Power. Practical Reform for Public Good. If elected to the Michigan House of Representatives, I want the people of Lansing and the surrounding communities to feel that government is becoming more honest, more useful, and more rooted in the real pressures they face every day. Big reform ideas should mean something in your rent, your paycheck, your child’s school, your trust in government, and your ability to imagine a stable future here.

Why start with Lansing?

Reform has to become real somewhere. It has to touch the lived experience of the people being governed.

Lansing is not just another place on a map. It is the state capital, a working community, and a place where the strengths and failures of Michigan government are felt close to home. People here live with the effects of housing costs, school policy, wage pressure, public trust, infrastructure quality, healthcare access, and whether the political system feels like it belongs to them or to the interests closest to power. That is why Lansing is the right place to begin proving that government can still work for ordinary people.

The first-term Lansing reform agenda

If elected, I want to focus early on 3 priorities that are realistic for a state representative to fight for, easy for constituents to understand, and meaningful enough that people can feel the difference: cleaning up state power, making housing more stable, and building schools around dignity and opportunity.

Priority 1: Clean Up State Power

If government writes rules for everyone else, government should be willing to live under stricter rules itself.

What I would push

I would push for stronger ethics and disclosure laws, better lobbying transparency, tighter conflict-of-interest rules, stronger whistleblower protections, and clearer public records around state contracting, influence, and decision-making.

My broader anti-corruption framework is built around the principle that systems should assume corruption is possible and be designed to make abuse harder, easier to detect, and more costly to attempt.

What this means for Lansing

Because Lansing is the state capital, people here live closest to the machinery of influence, contracting, lobbying, and insider politics. They should be the first to benefit from making that machinery cleaner and more accountable.

What people could actually feel

More visible public decisions. Less tolerance for insider self-dealing. More trust that state power is not simply being traded among well-connected people behind closed doors.

Why it matters

People cannot trust a government that keeps asking for patience while protecting the conditions that make corruption possible. Cleaning up state power is one of the clearest ways to show that reform is serious.

In plain terms

If you live in Lansing, you should not have to wonder whether the system around you is serving the public or serving itself. Government should feel more visible, more accountable, and less like a private club with public authority.

Priority 2: Make Housing More Stable

A home should feel like security, not a monthly question mark.

What I would push

I would push for stronger housing stability measures, more room for practical housing expansion, anti-speculation pressure where appropriate, stronger renter protections, and better pathways into first-time ownership, co-op models, and neighborhood-rooted housing.

My broader housing framework treats housing as an essential need, not just a speculative asset, and supports stronger baseline housing access, anti-hoarding and anti-vacancy pressure, and more stable housing delivery systems.

What this means for Lansing

Lansing residents should be able to imagine staying in their neighborhoods, building stability, and not being constantly pushed around by unstable rents, neglected housing, or speculative pressure.

What people could actually feel

More predictable housing conditions. Better protection against neglect and instability. Stronger support for people trying to move from renting without security toward some form of rooted long-term housing.

Why it matters

Housing pressure affects almost everything else: school stability, family finances, mental health, local business life, and whether people feel like they can build a future where they live.

In plain terms

People should not feel like their home is the most unstable part of their life. Housing policy should help people stay rooted, not make them feel disposable to investors, vacancy games, or rising instability.

Priority 3: Build Schools Around Dignity and Opportunity

Schools should open doors for young people, not train them to expect exclusion.

What I would push

I would push for school reform that limits harmful punitive discipline, supports student dignity, strengthens tutoring and support systems, expands apprenticeship and certification pathways, and helps public education focus more on growth, preparation, and real capability than on fear and exclusion.

My policy documents already support stronger protections against corporal punishment, zero-tolerance practices, discriminatory school treatment, and weaker one-size-fits-all education models, while expanding more balanced and humane pathways through school.

What this means for Lansing

Lansing families deserve schools that help students build futures, not systems that too often respond to difficulty with punishment, exclusion, or narrow measures of worth.

What people could actually feel

More confidence that schools are trying to help students succeed. Better pathways into trades, college, certifications, and work. More support for students who need help instead of being written off early.

Why it matters

The quality of a school system is not just about test scores. It is about whether young people leave it with dignity, confidence, skills, and real opportunities to build stable lives.

In plain terms

Families should be able to look at their schools and feel that they are preparing young people for life, not just disciplining them into silence or sorting them too early into failure.

How I would try to move reform in Michigan

Real reform requires legislation, public pressure, coalition-building, and a willingness to explain how policy affects daily life.

Push legislation where possible

Introduce, support, and publicly argue for bills that align with transparency, labor dignity, housing stability, educational opportunity, and public accountability.

Build issue-based coalitions

Work with labor, educators, families, reform advocates, and community organizations to make pressure for change broader than one office alone.

Keep the public informed

People should be able to understand not only what is being proposed, but what stands in the way, who benefits from the status quo, and what a better system would mean for their lives.

Translate policy into daily life

I want reform conversations to stay grounded in what they mean for rent, wages, schools, safety, public trust, and whether people feel more stable in the place they live.

Use office as a reform platform

State office should not only manage symptoms. It should help the public understand deeper structural causes and build momentum for broader change.

Prove better government is possible

The goal is not only to criticize what is broken. It is to help demonstrate that better rules, stronger institutions, and more honest public life can work in practice.

What I want Lansing constituents to feel

If I am doing this work well, I want people in Lansing and the surrounding communities to feel that someone is actually trying to connect policy to real life, explain the larger picture honestly, and push government toward serving people with more seriousness. I want constituents to feel heard, respected, and better able to imagine that public life can work differently than it does now.

This is the local beginning of a broader reform project

I am running for the Michigan House because reform has to start somewhere concrete. Lansing is not separate from the broader crises of trust, concentration, and institutional decline. It is one place where those pressures are visible every day. If we can make government cleaner, housing more stable, and schools more humane and opportunity-driven here, we can help Michigan become a model for broader change.
